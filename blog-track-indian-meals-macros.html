How to Track Calories in Homemade Indian Food Without Losing Your Mind

Roti, dal, sabzi, ghee, and dining out: the definitive guide to accurate macro tracking on an Indian diet with household measures.

By CaliPartner Editorial Team • Published November 2026 • 8 min read
Share with partner: WhatsApp Twitter / X

If you've ever tried logging a standard home-cooked Indian meal into a Western calorie tracker, you know the frustration: search for “1 bowl of Dal Tadka” and you'll find 50 different entries ranging from 90 kcal to 480 kcal. Search for “Roti” and you get everything from 60 calories to 220 calories with no explanation of size or flour type.

Most people give up after two weeks because they assume an Indian diet is “impossible to track.” But the issue isn't Indian food—it's that Western apps were built for pre-packaged, barcode-scannable meals. Homemade Indian cooking is fluid, spiced, and shared across families.

Here is the proven, simple framework to track Indian meals accurately, maintain sanity, and hit your fitness goals without cooking separate food from your family.

The 4 Golden Rules of Indian Macro Tracking

Rule 1: The Raw vs. Cooked Law

Lentils (dal, chana, rajma) and grains (atta, rice, oats) absorb massive amounts of water during cooking. 50g of dry raw moong dal yields nearly 140g to 160g of cooked dal depending on how thick your family prefers it. Water has zero calories, but it drastically alters cooked weight.

The Fix: For family meals, determine your standard household measure once. A typical Indian steel katori holds roughly 150ml to 180ml of cooked dal, which corresponds to ~30g of dry raw dal (~105 kcal, 7g protein, 18g carbs, 0.5g fat before tadka oil).

Rule 2: Standardise the Daily Phulka

You do not need to weigh every single roti before putting it on the tawa. In 90% of Indian households, a medium-sized handmade wheat roti (phulka) rolled from a 30g dough ball contains:

  • Calories: 75 – 85 kcal
  • Carbohydrates: 15 – 17g
  • Protein: 2.5 – 3.0g
  • Fat: 0.4 – 0.6g (without added ghee)

If you brush each roti with 1/2 teaspoon of ghee, add 22 kcal (2.5g fat) per roti. Tracking by roti count rather than gram weight is 95% as accurate and takes 3 seconds.

Rule 3: The “Tadka Offset” Method

The biggest hidden source of calories in Indian cuisine is the cooking oil or ghee used for tadka (tempering). Spices (cumin, turmeric, coriander, chilli powder) have negligible calories. The oil does not.

How to track shared pots: Ask whoever is cooking how much oil was used for the entire dish. For example:
• 2 tablespoons of mustard oil = ~240 kcal.
• If the pan yields 4 servings of sabzi, each person's portion contains approximately 60 kcal of cooking oil (6.5g fat) on top of the vegetables.

Rule 4: Solving the Indian Vegetarian Protein Puzzle

Traditional Indian vegetarian meals (roti, sabzi, dal) are carb- and fat-dominant. While dal is often praised as a protein source, it has a 3:1 carb-to-protein ratio. To hit 120g to 150g of daily protein on an Indian diet without exceeding your calorie limit, anchor your meals around high-density sources:

Food Item Serving Size Calories Protein Carbs Fat
Paneer (Raw/Low Fat) 100g 265 / 180 kcal 18g / 25g 2g 20g / 6g
Soya Chunks (Dry) 50g 170 kcal 26g 16g 0.5g
Curd / Dahi (Low Fat) 200g 120 kcal 8g 10g 4g
Yellow Moong Dal (Cooked) 1 medium katori (180g) 135 kcal 8g 20g 2g
Handmade Phulka Roti 1 roti (medium) 80 kcal 2.8g 16g 0.5g
Cooked Basmati Rice 1 medium katori (150g) 195 kcal 4g 42g 0.6g
Pro-Tip: Adding 50g of boiled soya chunks or 1 scoop of unflavoured whey to your dal or kneading whey into your roti dough can effortlessly boost your lunch protein by 25g with zero extra prep time.
